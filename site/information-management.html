1. Data and information management

Every sale, delivery and customer chat creates data. Managing it well means capturing it once, storing it in one trusted place, and turning it into information that helps you decide.

Dashboards and data visualisation

A dashboard shows your few most important numbers on one screen. Pick charts to match the question: a bar chart to compare products, a line chart for trends over time, and a single big number for a target such as cash at bank. Avoid decoration. If a chart does not change a decision, remove it.

Information quality

Information quality is how fit for use your information is. Poor quality is the quiet reason most small-business systems fail: a fancy dashboard built on bad data simply shows wrong answers faster.

The six qualities to check

QualityQuestion to askRetail example of failureConsequence
AccuracyIs it correct?Stock shows 40 tins; shelf holds 4Lost sales, wrong orders
CompletenessIs anything missing?Customer record has no phone number or consentCannot contact or legally market to them

Practical guidance: a quality routine for a small shop

  1. Name an owner. One person is responsible for each data set (stock, customers, prices).
  2. Capture it once, at the source. Scan barcodes at the till and goods-received desk instead of retyping.
  3. Use rules. Drop-down lists, required fields, and a standard date format (YYYY-MM-DD) stop errors on entry.
  4. Count and compare. Do a weekly cycle count of your 10 fastest products and compare with the system.
  5. Clean on a schedule. Monthly, merge duplicates and delete records you no longer need.
  6. Train staff on why. People enter better data when they see how the numbers are used.
Trade-off: perfect data is impossible and expensive. Aim for “good enough for the decision”. Be strict about money and stock, relaxed about nice-to-have details.

Measuring quality

You cannot improve what you do not measure. Pick a few simple scores, calculate them the same way every month, and compare them with a target.

ScoreHow to calculate itExampleTarget

Stock accuracy over six months (sample data)

A weekly cycle counts and a named stock owner lifted accuracy steadily. A rising line shows the routine is working, and a flat or falling line is an early warning.

Keep it simple: measure on the same day each month, write the scores on one page, and share them with staff. Celebrate improvement and fix the weakest score first.
